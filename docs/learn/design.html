Designing a multirotor

Fundamentals, chapter 3

Designing a drone is a balancing act. Every part adds weight. Weight needs thrust. Thrust needs power. Power needs a bigger battery, which adds weight again. This chapter gives the few rules that keep the circle under control.

Start from the job

Ask three questions first. What must it carry? How long must it fly? How much wind must it handle? The answers set the payload, the battery, and the thrust. The rest follows.

Weight, thrust, and the thrust-to-weight ratio

Add up everything that flies: frame, motors, electronics, battery, and payload. That is the all-up weight. Then add up the maximum thrust of all the motors. Divide one by the other.

thrust-to-weight ratio = total maximum thrust / all-up weight

RatioWhat it means
Below 1.5Too heavy. It may lift off, but it cannot correct itself or fight wind.
About 2The practical minimum. It hovers at half throttle, which leaves the other half for control and wind.
2 to 3A good range for camera, mapping, and spray drones.
Above 4Agile and fast, as in racing drones. Flight time is short.

The drone in the trainer weighs 1.2 kg and has a ratio of 2.6. Each of its four motors can lift about 0.78 kg.

What payload costs

Payload hurts twice. It lowers the thrust-to-weight ratio, so the drone handles worse. It also raises the power needed to hover, so the drone flies for less time. The power grows faster than the weight:

hover power = (weight)1.5 / √(2 ρ A) / efficiency

Here ρ is the air density and A is the total area swept by the propellers. Two lessons come out of this formula. Doubling the weight needs almost three times the power. Larger propellers lower the power, which is why drones that lift heavy loads have big, slow propellers.

Flight time

flight time = usable battery energy / average power

Battery energy in watt-hours is capacity in amp-hours times voltage. Plan to use about 80 percent of it and land with the rest. A bigger battery adds time, but it adds weight too, so each extra pack gives less than the one before. Past a certain size, a bigger battery makes the flight shorter.

Balance

Place heavy parts, above all the battery and the payload, so that the balance point sits at the center of the motors. If it does not, some motors work harder than others all the time. That wastes energy and leaves less in reserve for control.

How many rotors?

More rotors lift more and add safety. A quadcopter cannot fly with one motor out. A hexacopter can often limp home. An octocopter usually can. The price is weight, cost, and complexity.

The reason for the split is simple. A wing gets lift from forward motion, which costs little power. A rotor must make all of its lift by pushing air down, which costs a lot. So wings win on endurance and area covered, and rotors win on hovering, precision, and working in tight spaces.

A short design checklist

  1. Write down the payload, the flight time, and the wind limit the job needs.
  2. Estimate the all-up weight. Choose motors and propellers for a thrust-to-weight ratio of 2 or more at that weight.
  3. Choose the largest propellers the frame allows. Match the motor KV and the battery voltage to them.
  4. Size the battery for the flight time, plus a 20 percent reserve. Then recompute the weight and check the ratio again.
  5. Check that the speed controllers and the wiring can carry the peak current.
  6. Place the battery and the payload so the drone balances at its center.
  7. Test in steps: on the bench with no propellers, then a tethered hover, then free flight in calm air.
