Fundamentals: size a multirotor from its mission

Tell the tool what the drone must do. It sizes the propellers, motors, speed controllers, battery, and frame, shows every formula it used, and lets you change every assumption. Then check the result against real parts from a data sheet.

The page opens with a worked example: carry 0.5 kg for 20 minutes. Change the numbers to design your own.

What this is. A first-pass sizing tool for learning, based on momentum theory and typical component data. It gets you into the right range. It does not replace manufacturer thrust tests, and it is not a build guide.

1. What the drone must do

No design closes. With these numbers, the battery needed to lift the battery keeps growing. Ask for a shorter hover time, a lighter payload, more motors, or a better battery (raise the specific energy under the assumptions).

4. Principles and formulas

The tool repeats one loop until the numbers stop changing: guess the weight, size every part for that weight, add the parts up, and use the sum as the next guess. Aircraft are sized this way in practice, because every part depends on the total.

5. The assumptions, all adjustable

These values are typical, not exact. Change one and see how far the design moves. That is the honest way to learn which numbers matter.

What the tool leaves out

  • Forward flight, wind, and maneuvering all use more power than hover. Expect real flight times 10 to 30 percent shorter.
  • Propellers of the same size differ. Pitch, blade shape, and stiffness change the thrust and the current.
  • Battery voltage sags under load and in the cold, so peak thrust falls as the pack empties.
  • Vibration, cooling, center of gravity, wiring layout, and failsafe behavior decide whether a design flies well. None of them appear in these formulas.
  • Rules. Many countries set weight limits, for example 250 g and 25 kg, that change what you must do to fly legally.
